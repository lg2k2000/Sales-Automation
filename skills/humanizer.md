# Humanizer: voice rules + AI-pattern removal

Loaded by every routine that drafts external comms (`coworker`, `daily-review`). Read at the top of any session that writes emails or texts.

Two rules at once: **be polite** AND **don't sound AI-polished**. Not either/or.

- **Polite** means peer-asks-peer-for-help, not boss-issuing-orders. Direct AND warm. Never demanding ("do X. cut Y."). Reframe as questions and requests ("could you", "would you mind", "happy to talk through it").
- **Not AI-polished** means: no customer-service openers, no inflated significance, no rule of three, no em dashes, no buzzword soup, no signposting ("let's dive in"), no "I hope this helps." All the patterns below.

Read drafts aloud. If it sounds like a person texting a peer, it's good. If it sounds like a bot impersonating a person, redo.

## Process

For every draft:
1. Identify AI patterns (the 29 below)
2. Rewrite problematic sections
3. Preserve meaning, match Liam's voice
4. **Final anti-AI pass:** ask "what makes this so obviously AI?" Answer briefly. Then "now make it not obviously AI." Revise.

## CONTENT PATTERNS

### 1. Inflated significance / legacy / broader trends
**Words to watch:** stands as, is a testament/reminder, pivotal/key/crucial moment, underscores its importance, reflects broader, shaping the, marking a shift, evolving landscape, focal point, indelible mark, deeply rooted

AI puffs up importance by claiming arbitrary things contribute to a broader topic.

> Bad: "The meeting marked a pivotal moment in the evolution of our partnership, underscoring the importance of cross-functional alignment."
> Good: "We agreed on the next two action items. I'll send a one-pager by Friday."

### 2. Notability and media coverage claims
**Words to watch:** independent coverage, major outlets, leading expert, active social media presence

> Bad: "Our team has been featured in The Wall Street Journal, TechCrunch, and Forbes."
> Good: "Wired ran a piece on us last March."

### 3. Superficial -ing analyses
**Words to watch:** highlighting, underscoring, emphasizing, ensuring, reflecting, contributing to, fostering, encompassing, showcasing

AI tacks present participles onto sentences for fake depth.

> Bad: "We discussed the proposal, highlighting key concerns and ensuring alignment on next steps."
> Good: "We discussed the proposal. You raised two concerns. Next step is the addendum."

### 4. Promotional / advertisement language
**Words to watch:** boasts, vibrant, rich (figurative), profound, exemplifies, commitment to, nestled, in the heart of, groundbreaking, breathtaking, must-visit, stunning, enhancing

> Bad: "Our groundbreaking platform exemplifies a commitment to seamless customer experiences."
> Good: "The platform consolidates four CRM tools into one."

### 5. Vague attributions / weasel words
**Words to watch:** Industry reports, observers have cited, experts argue, some critics, several sources

AI attributes opinions to vague authorities.

> Bad: "Experts believe AI will transform sales workflows."
> Good: "Gartner's 2025 sales technology report says..." (and only if you can cite the actual line)

### 6. Formulaic Challenges/Future sections
**Words to watch:** Despite its X, faces several challenges, Despite these challenges, Future Outlook, Challenges and Legacy

> Bad: "Despite its strong revenue growth, the company faces challenges typical of fast-scaling SaaS startups. Despite these challenges, the future remains bright."
> Good: "Revenue is growing 80% YoY but churn ticked up 4 points last quarter."

## LANGUAGE AND GRAMMAR PATTERNS

### 7. Overused AI vocabulary

**Banned words:** delve, tapestry, supercharge, foster, nuance, plethora, leverage, robust, holistic, seamless, streamlined, elevate, unlock, empower, harness, navigate, journey, additionally, align with, crucial, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, garner, highlight (verb), interplay, intricate, intricacies, key (adjective), landscape (abstract), pivotal, showcase, testament, underscore (verb), valuable, vibrant.

These words appear far more often in post-2023 AI text than in real human writing.

### 8. Copula avoidance (avoiding "is" / "are")
**Words to watch:** serves as, stands as, marks, represents, boasts, features, offers

> Bad: "Our follow-up workflow serves as the foundation for the entire sales motion."
> Good: "The follow-up workflow is the foundation."

### 9. Negative parallelisms / tailing negations
"Not only X, but Y." "It's not just A, it's B." Tailing fragments like "no guessing" or "no wasted motion."

> Bad: "It's not just an email tool, it's a way to close more deals."
> Good: "The tool drafts follow-ups."

> Bad: "The system sends drafts to your inbox, no manual work needed."
> Good: "Drafts land in your inbox. You click Send when ready."

### 10. Rule of three
AI forces ideas into groups of three to seem comprehensive.

> Bad: "Our service offers speed, accuracy, and reliability."
> Good: "We deliver in 24 hours."

### 11. Synonym cycling (elegant variation)
> Bad: "The protagonist faces challenges. The main character must overcome obstacles. The central figure eventually triumphs."
> Good: "The protagonist faces challenges but eventually wins."

### 12. False ranges
"From X to Y" where X and Y aren't on a real scale.

> Bad: "Our experience spans from startups to Fortune 500s, from product to GTM."
> Good: "We've worked with startups and large enterprises on both product and go-to-market."

### 13. Passive voice / subjectless fragments
> Bad: "No configuration needed. The data is preserved automatically."
> Good: "You don't need to configure anything. We preserve the data."

## STYLE PATTERNS

### 14. Em dash overuse (—)
**Banned across the board.** Use periods, commas, or parentheses.

> Bad: "The deal is solid — but procurement is dragging — even after we sent the contract."
> Good: "The deal is solid, but procurement is dragging even after we sent the contract."

### 15. Boldface emphasis overload
> Bad: "We need to align on **objectives**, **timeline**, and **budget**."
> Good: "We need to align on objectives, timeline, and budget."

### 16. Inline-header vertical lists
> Bad:
> - **Speed:** We're fast.
> - **Quality:** Our work is good.
> - **Price:** We charge fair rates.
>
> Good: "We're fast, the work is good, and the price is fair."

### 17. Title Case in headings
> Bad: ## Strategic Negotiations And Global Partnerships
> Good: ## Strategic negotiations and global partnerships

### 18. Emojis in business comms
**Banned in emails.** OK in casual texts, never in email follow-ups.

### 19. Curly quotes
ChatGPT uses "..." (curly). Use "..." (straight). Most editors do this automatically; check on paste.

---

## COMMUNICATION PATTERNS

### 20. Chatbot collaboration artifacts
**Words to watch:** I hope this helps, Of course!, Certainly!, You're absolutely right!, Would you like..., let me know, here is a...

> Bad: "Here's a summary of the meeting. Let me know if you'd like more detail!"
> Good: "Quick summary of what we agreed today: ..."

### 21. Knowledge-cutoff / availability disclaimers
> Bad: "While specific details about the company's pricing are limited based on available information..."
> Good: Either find the information or skip the claim.

### 22. Sycophantic tone
> Bad: "Great question! That's an excellent point about pricing."
> Good: Just answer.

## FILLER AND HEDGING

### 23. Filler phrases
- "In order to" → "to"
- "Due to the fact that" → "because"
- "At this point in time" → "now"
- "In the event that" → "if"
- "Has the ability to" → "can"
- "It is important to note that" → just say it

### 24. Excessive hedging
> Bad: "It could potentially possibly be argued that the timing might have some effect on the outcome."
> Good: "The timing matters."

### 25. Generic positive conclusions
> Bad: "The future looks bright. Exciting times lie ahead as we continue our journey."
> Good: End on a concrete next step or just stop.

### 26. Hyphenated word pair overuse
**Words to watch:** third-party, cross-functional, client-facing, data-driven, decision-making, well-known, high-quality, real-time, long-term, end-to-end

AI hyphenates uniformly. Real writers don't. Drop most hyphens unless required for disambiguation.

### 27. Persuasive authority tropes
**Phrases:** the real question is, at its core, in reality, what really matters, fundamentally, the deeper issue, the heart of the matter

These pretend to cut through to a deeper truth, then restate the obvious.

> Bad: "At its core, what really matters is whether we can ship on time."
> Good: "We need to ship on time."

### 28. Signposting / announcements
**Phrases:** Let's dive in, let's explore, let's break this down, here's what you need to know, without further ado

Don't announce what you're about to do. Just do it.

### 29. Fragmented headers
A heading followed by a one-line paragraph that restates the heading before the real content begins.

> Bad:
> ## Performance
> Speed matters.
> When users hit a slow page, they leave.

> Good:
> ## Performance
> When users hit a slow page, they leave.

## PERSONALITY AND SOUL

Avoiding AI patterns is half the job. Sterile voiceless writing is just as obvious as slop.

**Have opinions.** Don't just report — react. "I'm not sure how to feel about this" beats neutral pros/cons.

**Vary rhythm.** Short punchy sentences. Then longer ones that take their time. Mix it up.

**Acknowledge complexity.** "This is impressive but also kind of unsettling" beats "This is impressive."

**Use "I" when it fits.** First person isn't unprofessional, it's honest.

**Let some mess in.** Tangents, asides, half-formed thoughts. Perfect structure feels algorithmic.

**Be specific about feelings.** Not "this is concerning" but "there's something off about how fast they pivoted on the price."

## Liam-specific voice rules

- Lowercase openers OK in iMessage. Email and Notion stay sentence-cased.
- Liam writes in fragments sometimes. Keep that.
- Specifics over abstractions. "Friday at 2" not "later this week". "$30k" not "in that price range".
- Read aloud. If you wouldn't say it to a friend over a beer, redo.

### Banned phrases beyond the 29 patterns above

**Customer-service openers:**
- "Just checking in"
- "Circling back"
- "Touching base"
- "Hope this finds you well"
- "Following up on our conversation"

**Commanding-the-recipient:**
- "Pick one"
- "Let me know"
- "Please advise"
- "Kindly"
- "Cut X" / "Pull Y" (declarative orders)

Reframe commanding language as questions:
- "Pick one" → "Which works for you?"
- "Let me know" → "Curious what you think"
- "Cut X" → "Could we drop X?"

### Scheduling: offer specific times, never ask

Liam's time is the constraint. Pull his calendar (Google Calendar `suggest_time` against his primary), pick 2-3 windows, **offer those windows**. Never ask "what works for you?" or "let me know your availability."

This applies in three places:
- **Email drafts** that propose a meeting, call, review, or any time-bound coordination.
- **Attio Tasks** where the next step is scheduling. Task content lists the specific offered windows, not "coordinate" or "find a time."
- **Notion plan entries** that capture scheduled work. List the offered windows so they're auditable.

> Bad: "Worth a quick review on Friday? Let me know what works."
> Good: "For a 20-min review Friday May 15: 10am CT or 2pm CT work on my end. Either fit?"

> Bad task: "[Counterpart] Coordinate dialer support availability for week of May 18."
> Good task: "[Liam] Send Miles three dialer-support windows from his calendar: Tue May 19 10am-12pm CT, Wed May 20 1pm-3pm CT, Thu May 21 10am-12pm CT."

Async fallback only if every offered window misses ("if none of these fit, happy to send it over and pick up async notes"). Still no open-ended ask. Liam never sends a Calendar invite autonomously (per CLAUDE.md hard NEVER); the routine surfaces the offered windows in a draft and a Task, Liam confirms with the counterpart and creates the invite.

## Bad → Good examples (Desk Monkey context)

> Customer-service mush:
> "Just wanted to circle back and see if you had a chance to review the proposal we discussed."
>
> Polite + concise:
> "Did you get a chance to read the proposal? Curious what your read is."

> AI buzzword soup:
> "I would love to leverage this opportunity to delve deeper into your team's workflow."
>
> Polite + concise:
> "Would love to walk through your team's workflow. 30 minutes next week?"

> Wishy-washy ask:
> "Let me know what works best for you and I'll send over a calendar invite."
>
> Polite + concise:
> "Tuesday 2pm or Wednesday 10am work on my end. Either of those open?"

> Demanding (sounds like an order):
> "Need 10 dropped to a top 10. Pulling Account Manager. Gun to your head, top 10 from these 19. Pick the ones that historically wrote checks. Cut everything else."
>
> Polite + concise:
> "I'd like to get to a top 10 if possible. Keeps the sequence focused. I'm dropping Account Manager too, which puts us at 19. Could you pick the 10 you'd keep? The ones that historically replied or wrote checks. No worries if it's hard to narrow. Happy to talk through it on a call."

> Demanding (giving orders to a referrer):
> "liam@deskmonkeyai.com is live now. Use this going forward, not my personal Gmail. Pull liamglennie@gmail.com off the invite and add liam@deskmonkeyai.com."
>
> Polite + concise:
> "Quick favor. liam@deskmonkeyai.com is finally live, so I'm trying to move off my personal Gmail. Would you mind swapping liamglennie@gmail.com for liam@deskmonkeyai.com on the invite? Appreciate it."

## Final anti-AI pass

Before sending any draft to Gmail Drafts:

1. Read aloud
2. Ask: "What about this would make a recipient guess it's AI?"
3. Answer briefly (3-4 tells, max)
4. Revise to fix those tells
5. Read aloud again

If pass 5 still has even one of the 29 patterns above, redo.
